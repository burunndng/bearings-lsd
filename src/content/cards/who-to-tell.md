There can be a strong pull to tell people about a meaningful experience —
partly to make sense of it out loud, partly because it feels too big to
keep entirely to yourself. That pull is understandable and doesn't need
justifying.

It's worth separating two different things before you talk, though:
wanting to share versus wanting someone specific to have this information.
Depending on where you live, who you work for, your custody situation, or
your immigration status, disclosure can carry real consequences that have
nothing to do with whether the experience itself was a good one.

A few questions that might help sort who to tell:

- Does this person actually need to know, or do you just want to tell them?
- Could this information be used against you — at work, in a legal matter,
  with family?
- Is there someone you trust who has some relevant context, rather than
  someone who would need everything explained and justified from zero?

There's also a specific trap worth naming: the urge to convince someone
else that this could help them too. That can come from a genuine wish to
share something good, but it can also land as pressure, and it isn't
yours to manage on their behalf. What was useful for you doesn't need
everyone else's agreement to have been real.
