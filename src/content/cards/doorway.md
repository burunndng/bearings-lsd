Some experiences leave a single image that keeps returning — a doorway, a
figure, a room, an animal, a scene. Not the whole experience, just one
picture that holds charge.

You may want to consider writing it down without explaining it. What did it
look like, what was around it, what was the light like? Leaving what it
means alone for now tends to hold more than deciding quickly.

If you want to stay with it a little longer, some people find it helpful to
let the image answer a couple of questions on paper, on its own terms:

- Who are you, as this image?
- What do you want?
- What do you fear?

There is no need to direct the answers. You may want to consider keeping the
exchange time-boxed — fifteen minutes with eyes open, writing — and stopping
while you still have choice about whether to continue. If an image starts to
feel overwhelming, or begins to feel like it *is* the truth of the
experience, you may want to consider setting it aside and returning to
ordinary things: rest, warmth, simple food, someone you trust.

That many people find an image helpful does not make it a diagnostic tool.
It is a way of holding what happened so it can be looked at. Early evidence
on journaling and on working with images after difficult experiences is
based on self-report, not on controlled trials specific to psychedelics.

If you want a private place, your [notes](/notes) live on this device and
go nowhere else. You can also skip this entirely — a good image, like a
good time, is allowed to simply have been there.

The four-lens page in [Learn](/learn/four-lenses-snapshot) offers three
other ways to look at the same experience, if you want them. You can use
one, several, or none.
